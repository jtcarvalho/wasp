# 1. Introduction

Surface gravity waves constitute one of the primary mechanisms through which momentum, energy, and gases are exchanged across the air–sea interface, exerting a fundamental influence on weather systems, climate variability, offshore operations, coastal engineering, and marine ecosystem dynamics. Their impacts are particularly significant in coastal environments, where wave-driven processes control shoreline evolution, sediment transport, coastal flooding, storm surge amplification, and the exposure of coastal communities to natural hazards. As more than one-third of the global population resides within coastal regions, accurate characterization of ocean wave conditions remains a scientific and societal priority.

Historically, sea-state characterization has relied on integrated bulk parameters such as significant wave height (Hs), mean wave period (Tm), and dominant wave direction. Although these parameters remain operationally relevant, they provide only a simplified representation of the ocean wave field. Oceanic wave conditions are inherently multimodal, frequently consisting of locally generated wind seas coexisting with one or more swell systems generated by distant storms. Under these conditions, bulk parameters may become physically ambiguous because they collapse multiple independent wave systems into a single statistical description, obscuring the underlying dynamical structure of the sea state.

Consequently, the directional wave spectrum has become the fundamental variable for describing and predicting ocean-wave conditions. Unlike integrated parameters, the directional spectrum explicitly resolves the distribution of wave energy as a function of frequency and propagation direction, providing direct access to the physical structure of the wave field. This information is particularly important for understanding the generation, propagation, transformation, and dissipation of swell systems, which can travel thousands of kilometers away from their generation regions while retaining a substantial fraction of their original energy.

Long-period swell represents one of the most efficient mechanisms for transferring wave energy across ocean basins. Generated by intense extratropical cyclones and other energetic atmospheric systems, swell propagates far beyond the forcing region and frequently reaches distant coastlines where it can dominate local wave conditions. Numerous studies have demonstrated that swell contributes significantly to coastal erosion, wave setup, harbor agitation, navigation hazards, and extreme coastal events. Consequently, understanding the evolution of individual swell systems is essential not only for wave forecasting but also for coastal risk assessment and climate-scale studies of wave variability.

The development of third-generation spectral wave models has significantly enhanced the capability to simulate these processes. Models such as WAVEWATCH III (WW3) explicitly represent wave generation, propagation, nonlinear interactions, and dissipation mechanisms, enabling the tracking of wave systems from their generation regions to remote coastal environments. Simultaneously, advances in remote sensing have provided unprecedented observational capabilities for monitoring wave spectra on regional and global scales.

Among these observational systems, Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) occupies a unique position because it provides quasi-global observations of directional wave spectra over remote ocean regions where in situ measurements are sparse or unavailable. SAR observations have enabled numerous advances in swell tracking, wave-model validation, and global wave-climate studies. However, SAR-derived wave spectra are affected by several physical limitations, including velocity bunching, hydrodynamic modulation, tilt modulation, azimuth cut-off effects, and directional ambiguities associated with the inversion process. These characteristics may introduce distortions that complicate the identification of physically meaningful wave systems.

The China–France Oceanography Satellite (CFOSAT), particularly through the Surface Waves Investigation and Monitoring (SWIM) instrument, represents a major advance in satellite-based wave observations. Unlike conventional SAR retrievals, SWIM was specifically designed to retrieve directional wave information over a broad range of wavelengths, providing observations that are particularly well suited for spectral validation studies. Complementarily, directional spectra reconstructed from National Data Buoy Center (NDBC) observations provide high-quality in situ measurements that serve as an important reference for evaluating both satellite products and numerical simulations.

Despite these advances in numerical modelling and observations, a critical challenge remains: the identification of physically coherent wave systems within directional spectra. This challenge is addressed through spectral partitioning, a procedure that decomposes a complex directional spectrum into individual wave systems that can be interpreted as distinct wind-sea and swell components. Spectral partitioning constitutes the foundation of numerous applications, including swell tracking, source-region identification, spectral data assimilation, model validation, wave-climate analyses, and the development of machine-learning models for sea-state classification and spectral reconstruction.

However, spectral partitioning remains one of the least explored sources of uncertainty in spectral wave analysis. Errors introduced during partition identification propagate directly into all subsequent analyses. Artificially splitting a single physical system into multiple partitions may lead to incorrect interpretations of spectral complexity, while excessive merging may conceal independent wave systems associated with different meteorological sources. Consequently, uncertainties associated with partition definition may become comparable to, or even larger than, uncertainties associated with the wave model itself.

The most widely adopted partitioning methodologies are derived from the watershed-based framework proposed by Hanson and Phillips (2001), which remains the de facto standard for directional-spectrum partitioning in both operational and research applications. The Hanson and Phillips methodology represented a major advance because it enabled the automated identification and merging of spectral systems based on physically meaningful criteria. Nevertheless, the original methodology was developed using datasets characterized by spectral properties that differ substantially from those available today.

Modern observing systems present distinct challenges that were not explicitly addressed in the original framework. SAR spectra may contain inversion artifacts and retrieval-related noise. CFOSAT spectra exhibit different spectral resolutions and directional characteristics. NDBC spectra reconstructed through Maximum Entropy Methods may display artificial partition fragmentation due to directional discretization limitations. Similarly, numerical models frequently produce smoother spectra than observations, reducing the visibility of secondary systems. Consequently, applying a single partitioning strategy across all observing systems may introduce systematic biases in partition identification and correspondence.

These limitations have important implications for contemporary applications. In spectral model validation, incorrect partition correspondence may lead to misleading conclusions regarding model skill. In data assimilation, partition ambiguity may contaminate the observational constraints used to update model states. In machine-learning applications, partition errors may propagate directly into training datasets, reducing the ability of algorithms to learn physically meaningful relationships between spectral structures and environmental forcing. Likewise, swell-tracking studies depend critically on the accurate identification and persistence of individual wave systems throughout their propagation pathways.

Building upon these challenges, the present study investigates whether adaptive partitioning strategies can reduce structural bottlenecks associated with spectral partitioning. Specifically, we evaluate a methodology based on percentile-preserving spectral-peak detection and source-dependent merge criteria designed to accommodate the distinct characteristics of SAR, CFOSAT, NDBC, and WW3 spectra. Unlike conventional approaches that focus primarily on integrated wave parameters, the proposed framework explicitly targets the preservation of physically coherent wave systems and the reduction of partition ambiguity.

The central hypothesis of this work is that a substantial fraction of observation–model disagreement originates not from errors in integrated wave energy, but from deficiencies in the representation of spectral organization and wave-system identity. Under this hypothesis, improvements in partition correspondence should arise from enhanced preservation of physically meaningful spectral structures rather than from modifications to total spectral energy. If confirmed, this interpretation would imply that future advances in spectral validation, data assimilation, swell tracking, and machine-learning applications may depend as much on improvements in wave-system identification as on improvements in numerical wave modelling itself.

Within this context, the objectives of this study are threefold: (i) evaluate the sensitivity of spectral partitioning to percentile-based peak preservation and adaptive merge-factor selection; (ii) quantify how these methodological choices influence partition correspondence between observations and WW3 simulations; and (iii) investigate whether improvements in partition correspondence represent genuine improvements in physical realism. By addressing these questions, this work seeks to establish a physically consistent framework for multi-sensor spectral partitioning and to provide a robust foundation for next-generation spectral validation and data-driven ocean-wave applications.
