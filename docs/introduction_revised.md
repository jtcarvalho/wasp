# Introduction

Ocean surface gravity waves regulate exchanges of momentum, heat, energy, moisture, and gases across the air–sea interface, with consequences for weather, climate, upper-ocean mixing, ecosystems, and the oceanic boundary layer (Phillips, 1977; Komen et al., 1994; Cavaleri et al., 2007; Holthuijsen, 2007; Ardhuin et al., 2010). Their role is especially consequential in coastal settings, where waves influence shoreline change, sediment transport, harbour operability, flooding, and risks to coastal and offshore infrastructure (Dean and Dalrymple, 2002; Holthuijsen, 2007).

Sea state is commonly summarized by bulk parameters such as significant wave height ($H_s$), mean or peak period, and mean direction (Longuet-Higgins, 1952; Holthuijsen, 2007). These metrics are useful but can obscure the structure of multimodal seas because they combine locally generated wind sea and swell systems with distinct origins, propagation histories, and spectral properties (Pierson and Moskowitz, 1964; Young, 1999; Gerling, 1992; Hanson and Phillips, 2001). Consequently, sea states with similar bulk statistics may have substantially different wave-system compositions, limiting model validation, forecasting, coastal-engineering applications, and studies of air–sea interaction (Komen et al., 1994; Portilla et al., 2009).

Directional wave spectra, $S(f,\theta)$, describe the distribution of wave variance in frequency and direction and provide the appropriate framework for resolving individual wave systems (Longuet-Higgins et al., 1963; Holthuijsen, 2007). They support the identification and characterization of wind sea and swell, the analysis of energy propagation and nonlinear interactions, and the validation and assimilation of third-generation spectral wave models (Hasselmann et al., 1973; Hanson and Phillips, 2001; Tolman, 2009; Ardhuin et al., 2010; WAVEWATCH III Development Group, 2019). This spectral perspective is essential for cyclone-generated swell, whose frequency dispersion and directional spreading modify the signal during propagation over thousands of kilometres and can create hazardous conditions far from the generating storm (Jiang et al., 2016; Jiang, 2019; Cavaleri et al., 2012).

Global spectral models are particularly valuable because they represent the full life cycle of swell systems without discontinuities at nested-domain boundaries and provide the wave-related fluxes needed by coupled atmosphere–ocean models (Tolman, 2009; WAVEWATCH III Development Group, 2019; Lionello et al., 1998; Breivik et al., 2015; Grayek et al., 2023). Their evaluation nevertheless requires observations beyond the sparse in situ network, whose highest density is largely confined to developed coastal regions, including the National Data Buoy Center (NDBC) network (Steele et al., 1992). Satellite observations therefore provide indispensable coverage over data-sparse ocean basins (Chapron et al., 2001; Quilfen and Chapron, 2019).

Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) has, for more than three decades, provided routine two-dimensional directional wave spectra at high spatial resolution and near-global coverage in most weather conditions (Hasselmann and Hasselmann, 1991; Engen and Johnsen, 1995; Chapron et al., 2001). CFOSAT, through the Surface Waves Investigation and Monitoring (SWIM) instrument, was the first mission specifically designed to provide routine global directional spectra together with collocated wind observations (Hauser et al., 2019; Hauser et al., 2021). These complementary observations have strengthened model evaluation, data assimilation, and operational forecasting; assimilation of CFOSAT observations in WAVEWATCH III-based systems has improved significant-wave-height and peak-period predictions. SWOT further expands satellite capabilities for observing long ocean waves and coastal dynamics (Ardhuin et al., 2023).

The interpretation of SAR spectra remains challenging. Tilt modulation, hydrodynamic modulation, and velocity bunching produce a nonlinear imaging mechanism, and velocity bunching can generate an azimuth cut-off that attenuates short-wave energy; under energetic conditions, this cut-off may exceed 450 m (Alpers and Hasselmann, 1982; Hasselmann and Hasselmann, 1991; Kerbaol et al., 1998; Wang et al., 2020). SAR spectra also have a 180° directional ambiguity and may contain signatures from currents, atmospheric fronts, rain, ships, and other non-wave processes (Chapron et al., 2001; Collard et al., 2009). These effects complicate direct comparison with model and buoy spectra.

Spectral partitioning addresses multimodality by decomposing a directional spectrum into physically meaningful wave systems that can be independently characterized, tracked, assimilated, and compared with model predictions (Gerling, 1992; Hanson and Phillips, 2001; Portilla et al., 2009). Watershed-based approaches are widely used because they efficiently separate neighbouring spectral maxima. However, noise and natural variability can create spurious maxima, causing over-partitioning and unstable boundaries; empirical merging and rejection rules also require source-dependent tuning (Portilla et al., 2009; Portilla et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023). The problem is amplified for SAR, where azimuth cut-off and imaging artifacts can elongate, split, or create apparent spectral peaks. Fixed thresholds therefore seldom transfer reliably among SAR, buoy, and model spectra (Hasselmann and Hasselmann, 1991; Chapron et al., 2001; Collard et al., 2009; Portilla et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023; Portilla et al., 2025).

Recent work has combined adaptive thresholds, physical constraints, wave-climate information, statistical learning, and machine-learning-based quality control to improve partitioning and cross-platform comparison (Stopa et al., 2016; Mazzaretto et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023; Portilla et al., 2025). Yet important model–observation differences remain: MACS-based analyses indicate that WAVEWATCH III often overestimates short-wavelength spectral energy, while long-period swell biases vary regionally (Li et al., 2021; Khan et al., 2021). Robust, objective, and transferable partitioning is thus still needed for intercomparing heterogeneous directional spectra.

This study introduces an enhanced adaptive spectral-partitioning framework that extends the classical watershed approach through: (i) adaptive percentile-based peak detection, (ii) Secondary Peak Reassessment (SPR), (iii) physically constrained partition merging, and (iv) a Physics-Constrained Spectral Partition Matching (PCSPM) strategy for model–observation intercomparison. The framework is evaluated with Sentinel-1 SAR, CFOSAT/SWIM, NDBC buoy, and WAVEWATCH III directional spectra to assess improvements in robustness, automation, and physical consistency across multimodal sea states.
